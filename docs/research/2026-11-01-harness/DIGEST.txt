MODEL: Levels per game = window / clock per level, and the clock binds: 76% of each game's window goes to levels it clears (12 to 13 min each), and the rest goes to the level still in play at the close. Throughput is at its floor, so tokens are not the scarce input. Correct theories per second are. Execution is cheap: multi-action plans fail about 8% of the time, and actions sit at 0.64x human, below the 1.15 cap on 78% of cleared levels. So clock per level is set by discovery: how fast wrong rules and wrong goals die. A hypothesis dies only on a counterexample, and counterexamples come from three sources at very different prices:
- the recorded history: zero actions, one tool call, seconds;
- a probe: 1 or 2 actions, about 33 s turns;
- prose deliberation: 162 s idle turns and 12K-token cut replies, with no ground truth.

Our model mostly buys the most expensive one. It defines a simulator in 20 to 35% of level plays but replays it against history in 6%. It checks goal guesses against history 0.3 times per run. It hand-simulates in 31 cut replies per run. Long levels (27% of clears, 66% of their clock) first show the winning code with only 13% of the level's clock left. The stuck modes agree: in 10 of 30 stuck levels a false rule came from one try, and the log already contradicts such rules. The lever is to make falsification cheaper than deliberation:
- compute it for the model (one-call replay of the model's own simulator and goal test against the log);
- intervene at the exact moment it deliberates without testing (a cut reply).

Computed outputs (seg8, step-verify) moved this model; advice lines did not. Actions are slack, so a few probe actions are cheap. A broad push to act more endangers the 22% of levels near the cap.

check_model(step, observe=None, start=None, border=4, show=3):
- hist = runtime_globals['history']; i0 = start if given, else (runtime_globals.get('level_starts') or [0])[-1].
- Walk i over i0+1 .. len(hist)-1, capped to the last 400: before = hist[i-1].frame, after = hist[i].frame, a = hist[i].action, res = hist[i].result.
- Skip and count by reason: a RESET or res.get('automatic'); before.level != after.level; res.get('game_over') (a death auto-resets the board).
- With observe: compare step(observe(before), a) == observe(after).
- Without observe: step(before, a) may return
  - ascii text: compared on interior cells [border, H-border) against after.ascii rows;
  - a list-of-lists grid: compared on the interior against after._grid;
  - a dict {(r,c): v}: only claimed cells, str v against the ascii char, int v against the grid;
  - None: counted as not claimed.
- An exception in step or observe is a mismatch carrying 'ExcName: msg[:100]'. It is never raised.
- Stop at 8 s of time.monotonic() and say so.
- Print one report, at most about 300 tokens. Example: "check_model: 37 of 41 transitions on this level match (skipped: 2 deaths, 1 RESET). First mismatches: history[212] 'UP': 3 cells differ, (12,30) predicted 'b' got '.'; ...". With show=3, each mismatch lists up to 6 cells, or the predicted and actual repr cut to 160 chars.
- Return {checked, matched, skipped, mismatches}.

check_goal(is_goal, start=None):

The level did not end on any of them, so this goal test is wrong or incomplete, or those boards still needed one more action (such as a confirm press)." or "check_goal: false on all 58 recorded boards of this level, consistent so far."
- Return the indices.

[public-arc3] Public ARC-AGI-3 harnesses have converged on five mechanisms: (1) log every frame and let the agent query it, (2) check every hypothesis against all of the recorded history, (3) plan inside a simulator, (4) run plans step by step and stop at the first prediction miss, (5) treat every recorded board as a non-goal. The systems are Twin, Tycho, Kepler, arc-skill, Retrodict and the 4x4 ablation. Duck/Franzen plus our overlays already cover (1) and soft versions of (2) to (4). Our harness has nothing for (5): the system prompt never tells the model that every recorded board on a level is a certified negative for the goal.

In 6 recent runs (crude regex), the model defines about 15.5 goal-like functions per run and checks about 0.3 per run against history. It checks simulators more often: about 11 of 102 simulator snippets per run loop over history. Clearing runs write their own verify-and-stop runners (sk48 `runplan`) and sometimes split plans into chunks because the prompt says 30 s. The harness actually excuses action time up to 120 s.

Nothing public explains the gap to rank 5:
- No top-30 team has published.
- xz's posts are all about infrastructure.
- Turducken (rank 3), in its own note, says it prunes experts, then runs a "short round of extra training" (a heal fine-tune). It also does heavy prompt coaching and uses human-play recordings. So at least one team near the top does fine-tune.

Ruled out from public evidence:
- Structured notes: Franzen says disabling them was the biggest gain in that area.
- Death ledger and guards: inert on this stack (10-11 replay).
- Stall cutoff (-9.1) and more streams (29.90 vs 47.30): Francise Liang, forum 750885.
- Summary on trim: persistgym (arXiv 2610.26632) shows truncation costs almost nothing when facts can be looked up again, and our python history allows that.
- Quadratic-scoring prompt plus temp 0.55 (EAnge1o, one offline run): we already sit at 0.62 to 0.67x human actions, below the cap.
- Graph-explorer fallback: its action efficiency is near zero (Twin's assessment).

Recommendation for the single wave: one overlay, "verify", built from candidates 1, 2 and 3. Gate it on helper use plus no harm. None of these effects can be resolved with 3 runs.
  * Goal-consistency check (Twin's certified negatives) 
  * Checked plan executor run_checked(plan, predict), plus the host-time sentence 
  * Typed transitions (Tycho's evidence contract) 
  * Tried-once no-op ledger at stall checks (Just Explore and Turducken 'notebook of what it has tried') 
  * Salient-event summary at stall checks (Twin goal-discovery signals) 

[agent-literature] Across this literature one mechanism keeps showing up with large measured effects: the agent writes its theory of the game as code, the harness replays that code against every logged transition, and each mismatch comes back as a concrete counterexample. Twin (ARC-AGI-3 public 25) scores 93.3 with this loop. The same model and agent with the harness removed score 61.1. The authors credit most of the gain to validation being enforced by the harness rather than requested in the prompt. Kepler, Rodionov's ablation (exact replay verification ranks first in every setting), WorldCoder and PoE-World all use the same pattern. EMPA adds a second idea: an object interaction counts as known only once it has been seen in the relevant state, and exploration goals reopen when the agent's state changes. AutumnBench finds humans spend 12.5% of their actions each on resets and no-ops as experiments, while reasoning models spend under 7% on both together.

Our own transcripts show a gap here [verified, 3 seg8 runs, 8,437 unique code blocks]. The model defined a simulator in 269 blocks but checked one against the recorded transitions in only 53, in 5 of 25 games (sk48 26, lp85 17). It already runs checked execution by hand (445 blocks).

Our harness also marks an object type as tested after a single no-effect click, in any state. That is exactly the 'tested in the wrong state' and 'written off' modes (8 of 30 stuck levels).

Top pick, buildable in under 3 hours and testable offline:
- **check_model and check_goal:** two sandbox helpers, stdlib only, plus about 170 tokens in the cached prompt and one clause in the stall check.
- **EMPA-style edit to unstick2:** a type or action counts as tested only once it had an effect or was tried in 2 or more board states.

Both are observable-only, call no API and add almost no tokens per turn. The magnitudes from frontier-model papers do not carry over to Flash-Next at about 1.8K tokens per action. The level gain is very likely below what a 3-run wave can resolve, so gate on whether the mechanism fires and does no harm.
  * verify kit: check_model + check_goal (Twin/Kepler/WorldCoder replay validation as a one-call sandbox helper) 
  * EMPA-style state-conditioned untested list (edit to unstick2) 
  * halt-on-mismatch executor run_checked(plan, step, observe=None) 
  * SWE-agent-style informative errors for the most frequent snippet tracebacks 
  * cross-level insight or workflow memory (ExpeL / Agent Workflow Memory / Reflexion) | dead-end

[code-read] I rebuilt the pin's patched tree and rendered the real system prompt (21,663 chars). The hfix/b2 copy was gone, so the rebuild uses arm l1a1y8sv2r3kp12lf15us8 with 11 overlays. I then measured the three us8 replica runs: 75 game transcripts, 4,484 turns.

Where the clock goes [verified]:
- Idle turns are 13% of turns but 26% of the clock: a median of 162 s each, against 33 s for a one-action turn.
- The first turn of each level from level 2 on takes a median of 91 s with 2.3x the thinking of later turns, about 5% of the clock.
- 93 replies hit the 12K output cap (31 per run). They hold a median of 35K chars of hand-worked prose, each costs about 200 s before the next action, and they cluster in sp80, sk48, bp35 and s5i5.
- After a cut, the next message (the turn opener) never says the reply was cut. The model usually picks the same deliberation back up ("Let me run the decisive experiment... reconsider once more").

The biggest misstatement is the objective. The prompt's only goal is "Optimize for as few in-game actions as possible", backed by four more lines about saving actions. It never says that completing levels matters most or that time is the binding limit. Yet 78% of cleared levels already sit at the 1.15 score cap (median 0.66 of human actions).

Measured non-levers [verified]:
- Context drains (4 per game) show no burst of re-derivation afterwards.
- Wrong stale-state refusals cost about 0.27 turns per game.
- NameErrors, tool timeouts, truncations and retention rejections are all small.
- Only 3.7% of post-action turns open with a pure inspect-the-last-change call.
- "Written off" signals appear equally often in stuck and cleared levels, so a harness list of them would not tell the two apart.

UNDO is valid in 6 of 25 games but was used 8 times in 3 runs, while those games log about 10 RESETs per run.

Ranking: (1) a scoring and clock objective bullet, (2) a code-first line after a cut reply, (3) an UNDO probe line. Each is a small string-anchored overlay. No single 3-run wave can resolve them, so gate on mechanism counters plus no harm.
  * costmodel: state the real objective (completion first, clock binding, actions mostly slack) 
  * codefirst: say when a reply was cut at the output cap, and redirect it to code or a probe 
  * undoprobe: tell the model UNDO is a cheap in-state experiment when it is valid 
  * groupdiff: make frame_diff consistent with seg8 (8-connected objects) 
  * levelup context: precomputed object table plus how the level was won, in the level-start opener 
  * notonce: list actions and click types that were no-ops only once or in only one state 
  * levelclock: show minutes spent on this level in the state line 
  * drain-time opener compaction | dead-end

[transcripts] I parsed every analyzer turn in the 11 newest runs with scripts: 16,750 turns, 275 game plays, 1,387 cleared levels. Discovery is what limits us, not execution. Levels that took over 15 minutes are 27% of level-2-and-later clears but 66% of their clock. On those levels the winning code first appears with a median of only 13% of the level's clock left (code-similarity proxy, matched on 94 of 272 levels).

These are not bottlenecks:
- Plan failure: of 7,494 responses that ran 3 or more actions, 7% were stopped by the no-op guard and 1% hit game over.
- Action efficiency: the agent uses a median 0.64x human actions; the score sits 1.2 to 5.6 points under its cap.
- Re-deriving mechanics: 81% of level openings call functions written on earlier levels.
- Tool errors: 8.2% of calls, mostly the model's own bugs.

What I measured:
- Level openings: 17.3% of the clock on cleared level-2-plus levels passes before the level's first action (median 83 s, 5.2K tokens).
- Turns with no action: 13% of turns but 30% of the clock; 22% of them are cut off mid-thought.
- Context trims: 4.2 per game play, each keeps a median of 6 turns, and 54% drop the current level's opening. Long levels run 44% of their clock without it, but a natural experiment shows no harm.
- Thinking: 11% of thinking characters are paths traced by hand, 20% are coordinate-heavy lines.
- Checking a simulator against recorded history: only 6% of cleared levels do it, against 4% before unstick2's "replay through a simulator" prompt line.

The biggest finding is a flaw in how gates are read. 6 of 19 lf15-family runs started play 12 to 22 minutes before the server was ready. Raw, they average 120.2 levels against 127.5; on a common 5,800 s play window it is 116.5 against 116.2. On that window seg8 is the best arm (124.0).

Recommendation: read the last 3-run wave on the common window. Build check_model (a one-line replay of the level's transitions through the model's own simulator) as the harness change the numbers support. The trim ledger is second tier.
  * Aligned-window gate reading (a fix to how the last gate wave is read, not a harness change) 
  * check_model(read, step): one-line replay of the current level's recorded transitions through the model's own simulator 
  * Trim ledger: harness-computed level facts re-attached when a context trim drops the current level's opening 
  * search() helper for move planning 
  * Level carry-over line at each level start
